何谓均方误差

AE & MSE

1. 绝对误差（误差的绝对值）

（Absolute Error）

绝对误差是指预测值与实际值之间的差值，具体计算公式为：
$$
\text{绝对误差} = | \hat{y} - y |
$$
其中，$\hat{y} 是预测值，y 是实际值。$

绝对误差的意义是直接表示预测值与实际值之间的偏差大小，units与原始数据相同。绝对误差对于一些应用场景很有用，尤其是在我们关心预测的实际误差量时。

2. 均方误差

（Mean Squared Error, MSE）

均方误差是通过对每个数据点的误差的平方并取平均来==衡量模型预测的误差==，计算公式为：
$$
MSE =
\frac{1}{n}
\sum_{i=1}^{n}
(\hat{y}_i - y_i)^2
$$
其中，

  • $n$ 是样本数量，
  • $\hat{y}_i$ 是第$i$个数据点的预测值，
  • $y_i$ 是第$i$个数据点的实际值。

均方误差在衡量模型的预测效果时有以下特点：

  • 平方的作用：由于误差被平方，较大的误差会对MSE产生更大的影响。换句话说，MSE对较大误差特别敏感，尤其适合于需要惩罚大的误差的场景。
  • 单位：MSE的单位是原始数据单位的平方，这可能不如绝对误差直观，但可以更清晰地表现误差的规模。

Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and Mean Squared Error (MSE) are both widely used metrics for evaluating the performance of predictive models, but they have different characteristics that make MSE more suitable for certain applications than MAE. Here’s why MAE might not always be as good as MSE:

1. Sensitivity to Large Errors:

  • MSE ==penalizes== larger errors more heavily because it squares the difference between the predicted and actual values.
    This makes MSE more sensitive to outliers异常值 or large deviations大偏差 in the predictions.
  • MAE, on the other hand, treats all errors equally, whether they are large or small. It simply calculates the absolute difference between the predicted and actual values without amplifying larger errors.

Why does this matter?

  • If you want to detect and penalize large errors more, MSE is preferred because its squared nature emphasizes significant discrepancies.
  • In some applications, large errors might be particularly problematic, and using MAE may not reflect the severity of those issues adequately.

2. Mathematical Properties:

  • MSE is differentiable, which makes it mathematically easier to work with in optimization algorithms, especially when performing gradient-based methods like gradient descent梯度下降. The derivative of the MSE loss function is continuous and smooth, which helps in finding the optimal parameters of a model.
  • MAE, while still a valid loss function, is not differentiable at zero (where the predicted value exactly matches the true value), making it harder to optimize using methods like gradient descent. Instead, MAE requires more complex optimization techniques (e.g., linear programming or subgradient methods).

Why does this matter?

  • MSE is often preferred in machine learning, particularly when ==gradient-based optimization methods== are used because its differentiability可微分性 makes the optimization process more efficient and stable.
  • MAE can still be used in some contexts, but the non-differentiability ==introduces== challenges, especially for large datasets or more complex models.

3. Interpretability:

  • MAE provides a more interpretable result in the sense that it directly measures the average magnitude of the errors, making it easier to understand the typical error size in the same units as the original data.
  • MSE, however, gives the error ==in squared units==, which may not always be as intuitive. For instance, if you’re predicting a variable measured in meters, the MSE will be in square meters, which might be harder to interpret in practical terms.

Why does this matter?

  • If you need a straightforward, understandable measure of average error, MAE is more intuitive. However, when you need to emphasize large errors and want a function that works well with standard optimization techniques, MSE is often more desirable.

4. Robustness to Outliers:

  • MAE is more robust to outliers than MSE. Since it treats all errors equally, outliers have a smaller impact on the overall error measurement.
  • MSE, because it squares the errors, amplifies the effect of outliers, which can lead to an overestimation of the model’s error when the dataset contains extreme values.

Why does this matter?

  • If your dataset contains outliers that you don’t want to heavily influence the model evaluation, MAE might be a better choice. On the other hand, if you want your model to specifically perform well on all instances, including outliers, MSE will help emphasize their impact and drive the model to reduce those large errors.

5. Bias in Parameter Estimation:

  • MSE tends to be more biased towards larger values. In the context of linear regression, for example, minimizing MSE leads to a solution that emphasizes the minimization of large residuals, which can result in a model that doesn’t perform as well for smaller but more frequent errors.
  • MAE tends to give equal weight to all errors, meaning it may lead to a more balanced model that doesn’t disproportionately focus on large residuals.

Why does this matter?

  • If your data contains errors of varying magnitude and you want to avoid focusing disproportionately on larger errors, MAE may be preferred. However, if you need the model to focus on reducing large errors, MSE will guide the model in that direction.

Conclusion: Why MSE Is Often Preferred Over MAE

  • MSE is better for problems where large errors are particularly undesirable, as it gives more weight to large deviations and penalizes them more heavily.
  • MSE is also easier to optimize mathematically, especially when using gradient-based methods.
  • MAE is more robust to outliers and offers a more intuitive measure of average error, but it can be harder to optimize and doesn’t penalize large errors as effectively as MSE.

Ultimately, the choice between MAE and MSE depends on the problem at hand. If minimizing large errors is critical and mathematical ease of optimization is important, MSE is often the better choice. If robustness to outliers and interpretability is more important, MAE might be preferable.

expectation and variance

1. Expectation (Expected Value)

Expectation (also called the expected value or mean) is a measure of the central tendency of a random variable, representing the “average” outcome you would expect if you repeated an experiment many times.

  • If you have a random variable $ X $, the expectation of $ X $, denoted as $ E(X) $, is essentially the weighted average of all possible values of $ X $, weighted by their probabilities.

For ==discrete== random variables:

If $ X $ is a discrete random variable, then its expectation is calculated as:
$$
E(X) = \sum_{i} x_i \cdot P(x_i)
$$
where:

  • $ x_i $ is a possible value of $ X $,
  • $ P(x_i) $ is the probability of $ x_i $
Example:

Consider a fair均匀的公平的 six-sided六面 die骰子.

The random variable $ X $ ==represents== the outcome of a roll, so it can take values $ 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 $.

Since the die is fair, each outcome has a probability of $ \frac{1}{6} $.

The expected value $ E(X) $ is:
$$
E(X) = 1 \cdot \frac{1}{6} + 2 \cdot \frac{1}{6} + 3 \cdot \frac{1}{6} + 4 \cdot \frac{1}{6} + 5 \cdot \frac{1}{6} + 6 \cdot \frac{1}{6} = \frac{1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6}{6} = 3.5
$$
So, on average, ==you would expect== a roll of 3.5, which makes sense because it’s the “center” of the die’s values.

For continuous random variables:

If $ X $ is a continuous random variable, the expected value is the integral of the value of $ X $ multiplied by its probability density function $ f(x) $:
$$
E(X) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x \cdot f(x) , dx
$$
This formula is used in cases where the variable can take any value within a certain range, like height or weight.

2. Variance

Variance is a measure of how spread out or dispersed分散程度 the values of a random variable are from its expected value. In other words, it tells you how much the values tend to deviate偏离 from the average.

  • The variance of a random variable $ X $ is denoted as $ \text{Var}(X) $, and it is calculated as the average of the squared differences between each value of $ X $ and the expected value $ E(X) $.

For discrete random variables:

$$
\text{Var}(X) = E(\space\space(X - AVG)^2\space\space)
$$

This can also be computed using the formula:
$$
\text{Var}(X) = E(X^2) - AVG^2

\
AVG = E(X)
$$
where $ E(X^2) $ is the expected value of the square of $ X $.

For continuous random variables:

The formula for variance is similar:
$$
\text{Var}(X) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} (x - E(X))^2 \cdot f(x) , dx
$$
where $ f(x) $ is the probability density function of $ X $.

Example:
Using the same six-sided die example as above:

  • We already know that $ E(X) = 3.5 即 AVG=3.5 $.
  • Now we calculate $ E(X^2) $:
    $$
    E(X^2) = 1^2 \cdot \frac{1}{6} + 2^2 \cdot \frac{1}{6} + 3^2 \cdot \frac{1}{6} + 4^2 \cdot \frac{1}{6} + 5^2 \cdot \frac{1}{6} + 6^2 \cdot \frac{1}{6}
    \
    = \frac{1 + 4 + 9 + 16 + 25 + 36}{6} = 15.1667
    $$
    Now, the variance is:
    $$
    \text{Var}(X) = E(X^2) - AVG^2
    \= 15.1667 - (3.5)^2
    \= 15.1667 - 12.25
    \= 2.9167
    $$
    So, the variance of rolling a fair die is approximately $ 2.92 $.

3. Standard Deviation 标准差 $ \sqrt{\text{方差}} $

Standard deviation is simply the square root of the variance. It is also a measure of spread, but unlike variance, it is in the same units as the data (since variance is in squared units). It is often easier to interpret than variance.
$$
\text{Std}(X) = \sqrt{\text{Var}(X)}
$$

Why Are Expectation and Variance Important?

  • Expectation helps you understand the “center” or average of your data. It tells you what value you can expect on average when performing an experiment.
  • Variance gives you an idea of how much the data points vary from the expected value.
    • If the variance is high, the data points are ==spread out==;
    • if it’s low, they are ==clustered around== the expectation.
